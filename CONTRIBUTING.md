# Contributing to Frontic UI

## Your contribution starts with the problem

Frontic UI is built with its contributors. You'll find their names in our changes. What we ask of them is the part that's hardest to get right: **what** should change, and **why**.

That's why Frontic UI doesn't take pull requests from outside the team. Code got cheap: our coding agents turn a well-understood change into a component update in minutes. Seeing the right problem, in a real project, with the context that explains it, is still rare. That's the contribution that moves Frontic UI forward, and it's yours to make.

### What a strong contribution looks like

- **You hit a bug:** what you did, what you expected, what happened. Name the component and the version, and add a minimal reproduction.
- **Something's missing:** the job you're trying to get done, and where Frontic UI gets in your way. Describe the problem first. If you have a fix in mind, add it as one option, not the answer.
- **Your context:** your stack, your storefront, your constraints. A real use case beats a hypothetical one every time.

### What happens with your contribution

1. **The Frontic UI team picks it up.** A person on the team reads every issue.
2. **We get to the core.** What's the actual problem underneath? One component, or a pattern across many?
3. **We check the impact.** Who else does it affect? What does a change cost in API surface, accessibility, theming and upgrades?
4. **We work out the options.** Usually two or three, each with its trade-off, and the one we think is right for Frontic UI.
5. **We pitch a change proposal.** The product lead signs it off, sends it back with feedback, or declines it. You see the decision and the reason on your issue, and you're welcome to weigh in.
6. **We build it, and your name goes with it.** The team writes and reviews the code. The change credits you as the contributor who brought it.

### Why contributing works this way

A component registry lives on consistency: one API style, one way of handling accessibility, one theming model. A pull request written from one corner of the library can be good code and still pull the whole library in a different direction. Your issue lets us solve your problem the way the whole library needs it solved, and every contributor after you benefits from that.

Other projects have come to the same place. tldraw closed outside pull requests in January (["Stay away from my trash!"](https://tldraw.dev/blog/stay-away-from-my-trash)). Paul Stack put it plainly in ["The Community Pull Request Is Dead"](https://stack72.dev/the-community-pull-request-is-dead/): "The agents can write the fix. They need you to find the bug." Latent Space's ["PRs NOT Welcome"](https://www.latent.space/p/pr-not-welcome) covers Flue, tldraw, the Vercel AI SDK and Astro moving the same way. Andrej Karpathy, relaying Peter Steinberger, gave it a name: [the prompt request](https://x.com/karpathy/status/2040473058834878662).

There's a fair counterpoint. GitHub's ["Your contributors are AI-first now. Is your project?"](https://github.blog/open-source/maintainers/your-contributors-are-ai-first-now-is-your-project/) argues for keeping pull requests open behind good gates. That fits projects that grow mainly through outside code. For a design-led registry, what we need most from contributors is judgement and real-world context, not keystrokes.

### Our side of the deal

- **A person decides.** No agent builds from your issue directly. Someone on the team reads it, decides, and owns the change.
- **Every decision comes with a reason.** That goes for declined proposals too.
- **Docs and examples count.** Gaps there are contributions as well, and often the quickest to land.
